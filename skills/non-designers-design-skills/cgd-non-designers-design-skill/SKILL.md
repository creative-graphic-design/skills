# Create, review, and improve a design

Make the reader's intended first impression, reading order, and next action visible in the layout. Use the same design knowledge for making choices and diagnosing problems, guided by the user's purpose, audience, medium, and existing identity.

## Establish the task

Use the supplied content, audience, purpose, dimensions, medium, output format, and brand constraints. Inspect an existing artifact before reviewing or revising it. Resolve missing details only when they change the design substantially; otherwise state the assumption and proceed. Preserve required wording, contact details, and functional behavior.

Choose the work from the request:

| Request                                   | Action                                                                                    |
| ----------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Create a design                           | Produce the requested artifact or actionable layout specification.                        |
| Review a design                           | Inspect it and return findings and proposed changes. Do not edit the design.              |
| Improve a design, or create and refine it | Review the existing or first version, apply relevant corrections, and recheck the result. |

Use the available project tools and assets. A request for an artifact needs an artifact, not just advice. Do not select a new application or output format solely to demonstrate the principles.

## Inspect the visual examples

Before making or judging layout decisions, open the [original layout comparison](references/layout-comparison.png) with an image-capable tool. Compare reading order, distances within and between groups, shared edges, and repeated treatments. Before making or judging type combinations, open the [original type comparison](references/type-comparison.png) and compare the quiet single-family treatment, weak role distinction, and stronger role contrast.

Identify the relationship relevant to the brief; do not copy the examples' dimensions or style automatically. These examples change several variables together, so they do not isolate the effect of a single principle. Their editable sources are [layout SVG](references/layout-comparison.svg) and [type SVG](references/type-comparison.svg). If image inspection is unavailable, state that limitation instead of claiming to have examined an example.

## Proximity

Physical closeness suggests a relationship. Group the information by meaning before setting distances. Keep a heading closer to its content than to the preceding block, a caption with its image, and contact details with the action they support. Distinguish gaps within and between groups; equal gaps can be appropriate for peer groups. Use empty space to clarify relationships instead of filling every corner. Do not group unrelated content just to reduce the number of blocks.

## Alignment

Shared edges or baselines connect text, images, rules, and distant groups. Establish specific anchors, using a strong existing photo edge where useful. Look for edges that almost line up or nearby elements using unrelated axes. Centered composition can suit a formal brief; choose or preserve it deliberately instead of treating it as a defect. An intentional break should remain understandable against the established alignment system.

## Repetition

Repeated treatments make related content recognizable within a page and across a publication. Reuse heading styles, spacing relationships, accent colors, rules, and image treatments for equivalent roles. Strengthen an existing repeated element before adding decoration. A feature can differ against a stable pattern, but repeated decoration should not compete with the focal point.

## Contrast

Contrast distinguishes information roles and attracts attention. Unify equivalent roles; separate different roles through deliberate changes in size, weight, shape, spacing, direction, or color. Nearly identical treatments can look accidental. Choose the focal point from the communication goal: an event may lead with its subject and date, while a service listing may lead with the service. The organization name is not automatically the most important element. Making everything large and bold removes hierarchy.

## Combine the four principles

Trace the intended reading order, group the information, establish alignment anchors, repeat meaningful treatments, and make the role differences visible. Check the effect of each change on the other relationships. One defect may involve several principles; explain it once with the relevant reasoning instead of inventing a finding for every principle.

## Color

Start from the brand, an important image, or the intended mood. Choose a relationship that serves the piece: one hue with tints and shades, neighboring hues for continuity, or complementary, split-complementary, or triadic hues for more separation. Give colors roles such as background, text, and accent rather than equal visual weight by default.

Check light-dark separation as well as hue. Different hues with similar values can merge in grayscale or become difficult to read when adjacent. A warm accent can command attention with a small area; judge its actual effect against the background. Keep the primary emphasis recognizable when color is removed. A palette relationship alone does not establish readability, and an unmeasured screenshot does not establish a contrast ratio.

Use screen color for screen artifacts. For print, follow the printer's requested color space and output specifications; do not assume a monitor preview predicts ink on paper. Avoid repeated color-space conversions.

## Adapt to the medium

- For a flyer, poster, ad, or slide, make the main message easy to find at its expected viewing distance, then group supporting information and the next action.
- For business cards and stationery, carry the same identity across pieces. Keep contact information usable and leave letterhead space for the letter itself.
- For newsletters and multipage documents, repeat columns, headings, captions, and spacing. Let a feature differ against that stable pattern.
- For folded material, plan the panel reading order and keep essential text clear of folds and gutters. Verify dimensions against the production brief.
- For web pages, preserve navigation and component consistency, readable line lengths, and layout at the requested viewport sizes. Use the project's current layout methods.

Treat the book's particular font bans, point sizes, postal dimensions, and historical web techniques as examples of their original context, not universal production requirements. Do not infer print behavior or responsive behavior from a single preview.

## Type relationships

Distinguish intentional harmony from accidental similarity. A restrained, single-family composition can be the right answer. For a conflicting combination, identify the shared features before proposing a replacement. Add another family only when it contributes a distinguishable role; contrast does not require extra fonts or decoration.

## Typeface categories

Oldstyle serifs, modern serifs, slab serifs, sans serifs, scripts, and decorative faces provide useful Latin categories. Compare actual letterform construction rather than names alone: serif shape, thick-to-thin stroke change, stroke stress, width, and x-height determine whether two faces look confusingly similar. Two category labels alone do not prove a good pairing.

Start with the available family's real weights and styles. Check display faces with fine hairlines at the final size; script and decorative faces usually need short text and enough room. Set body text for sustained reading through suitable line length, spacing, and weight. For other writing systems, inspect actual glyphs, available weights, and mixed-script balance instead of imposing Latin-specific rules about capitals or italics.

## Type contrast

Use the six dimensions as choices, not a requirement to apply all six:

| Dimension | Design and review decision                                                                                                                                                             |
| --------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Size      | Separate information levels with a visible change; keep necessary details readable at the intended viewing size.                                                                       |
| Weight    | Use a real heavier or lighter cut to reinforce the hierarchy. Watch for a small bold element competing with a large light headline; making both more emphatic can worsen the conflict. |
| Structure | Compare clearly different constructions, such as an oldstyle serif and a monoweight sans, or distinct weights within one family. Switching between similar faces can add noise.        |
| Form      | Distinguish form from structure. Roman versus italic or mixed case versus capitals changes form; two flowing scripts, or a script and similar italic, may remain too alike.            |
| Direction | Contrast a horizontal heading with a tall text column. Rotate text only when the direction contributes to the message, not to fill an empty corner.                                    |
| Color     | Consider both hue and the light or dark texture made by weight, density, line spacing, letter spacing, and letterforms. A monochrome page can still have strong typographic contrast.  |

## Apply the knowledge

### Create

Use the brief to choose the focal point and reading order, then compose the artifact using the relationships above. Preserve the user's content and identity. Check the rendered result before delivery where tools permit.

### Review

Inspect the supplied artifact against its brief. Prefer the rendered page for visual claims, using editable source to locate causes and propose exact changes. When given only text or a layout specification, evaluate what it establishes and mark visual conclusions as provisional. Request the missing artifact if no meaningful assessment is possible.

Prioritize faults that obstruct the message or reading order before small refinements. Each finding identifies the location and observable condition, the resulting reader difficulty, and a specific revision. Use exact values when the source supports them; otherwise describe the relationship to change. Distinguish defects from optional stylistic alternatives. Do not invent findings to fill a quota. If the design satisfies the brief, say so and explain any remaining uncertainty. Return the critique without editing the design.

### Improve

For an existing design, begin with review instead of recreating it. For a new design requiring review and refinement, preserve a reviewable first version. Use the same brief through creation, review, and correction. The executing agent owns the result; a separate reviewer, when used, owns findings rather than edits. Give that reviewer this skill, the artifact, and the brief, not a desired verdict. If reviewing your own work, describe it as self-review.

Apply findings that affect communication, readability, or the brief. Do not implement a stylistic preference that contradicts a user constraint. If no material defect is found, keep the design. Recheck the affected areas and repeated treatments. Finish when the brief is satisfied and no actionable material finding remains. If another pass adds no useful improvement or needs a user decision, report the unresolved issue rather than looping indefinitely.

## Verify and deliver

For visual checks, inspect the rendered artifact at the intended size and as a reduced preview. Trace the focal point and reading order, inspect grouping and alignment, compare repeated treatments, and check that contrast distinguishes roles. Use a grayscale preview when hierarchy depends on hue. Check clipping, overlaps, missing text, and readability against the brief. Distinguish what was observed from measurements not taken; source-only checks are not visual verification.

Return the requested artifact, specification, or critique with a brief account of relevant decisions or revisions and unresolved findings or verification limits. Do not publish, send, or deploy the artifact unless the user requested that action.

## Source

Robin Williams, _The Non-Designer's Design Book_, third edition, 2008. The principles draw on chapters 1-8 for composition and color, and chapters 9-12 for typographic relationships and design practice.
